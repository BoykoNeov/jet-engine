The sized blades

Two limits set each spool's design blade speed: the airflow level (an efficiency target) and the blade material (the root of an untapered blade at its yield strength, brought down by the overspeed factor). The slower one wins. The stage count follows from the work the spool must do at that speed, rounded up to whole rows. The redline is the speed at which the blade root reaches its strength limit, divided by the overspeed factor.

Speeds are physical shaft speeds against design. The vane angle includes the design pre-swirl; rung 85's crossings sit near 66–69°, further than real vanes turn. "Never reached" rows are read at the last setting the search reached; along the way the other shaft can run faster than at that end (it did, mid-way, in a third of such cases sampled, never above its redline). The strength limit is optimistic (no taper, no disc, no hot-metal weakening, yield rather than breaking strength), so "crosses" is robust and "under" is one-sided. On the thermally perfect gas, the row-by-row levers still split the pressure rise between rows with a fixed γ of 1.4.

Temperature commanded makes the turbine-inlet temperature follow the move exactly, as textbooks do. Fuel metered moves the fuel flow instead, between the two steady fuel flows; the temperature is then whatever the burner gives. On a slam the shaft cannot yet pump the extra air, so the temperature overshoots. A real engine meters fuel. The map below shows each run's path; on a slam it swings toward the stall line. The stall margin tile reads the steady point only while the nozzle is choked (rung 36); here the flow coefficient itself is shown at every moment, beside the stall line you set, and the stretches where the nozzle is unchoked are drawn thin and dashed.

What is computed, and what is not. Each change runs the project's design-point cycle (build_turbojet(…).run(…), the same call the command line makes) for the gas model you pick: one engine with its compressor sized to your pressure ratio and its burner to your turbine inlet temperature. It is a design point, not a fixed engine being throttled. Flight altitude and air temperature/pressure are linked through the 1976 US Standard Atmosphere: type a pressure and the altitude becomes the pressure altitude. Type a temperature and the "off standard day" value follows. Above Mach 1 the inlet loses extra pressure through the model's supersonic recovery law.

The T–s diagram. Stations 0 and 9 are the still outside air and the exhaust jet (static states). Stations 2–5 are total (stagnation) states. Entropy is measured from the outside air. The air and the burned gas are different mixtures, so the jump across the burner mixes heat added with that change of mixture. The burner and the exhaust-cooling legs are drawn as curves: the shape is the burned gas's own constant-pressure line, and whatever that misses the next station by (the pressure loss, the change of mixture) is spread evenly along the way, so the curves meet the station points exactly. With the perfect gas, the textbook constants (cp = 1004, R = 287, γ = 1.4) do not quite agree with each other. An ideal compressor then shows a tiny, exactly predictable slope instead of a vertical line.

Fly it. The design's hardware is frozen: the turbine and nozzle throat areas, and the flow and speed it was designed for. Moving the throttle or the flight then lets the engine find its own pressure ratio, airflow and shaft speed (the project's rungs 31–36: a choked turbine, a fixed convergent nozzle that may unchoke at low power, and a representative compressor map). Capturing a throat area needs a convergent nozzle, so the frozen design is re-run with one; its numbers can differ from a "fully expanded" design. The exhaust pushes against the air pressure of the flight you set. The stall margin is rung 36's: it rests on a stall line you choose and a map shape you pick, so only its trend (thinner at low power) is meaningful. It is not modelled while the nozzle is unchoked.

Slam the throttle. The project's rungs 34 and 35: the shaft speed becomes something that changes in time, driven by the gap between the turbine's power and the compressor's, and the engine is stepped forward in small time steps. Time is counted in the shaft's own response time τ (its rotor inertia times the design speed squared, over the design shaft power); the model has no rotor inertia, so it cannot give seconds, and its findings are about how long the move takes compared with τ. A move must last a whole number of the model's 0.02 τ steps: one that ends between steps makes the peak temperature depend on the step size. When a run cannot continue, the page shows the points it has and why it stopped (some stops are limits of the model, said as such). The equilibrium gas is not offered here: the model cannot meter fuel on it.

Size the blades. Rung 85 on its own two-spool engine (a separate engine from Design's, which has one compressor). Each compressor's front row is sized from two limits, how fast the air may meet the blade tips (an efficiency target) and the blade material's strength at the root, and the stage count, blade speeds and the redline come out. A stator lever then holds the front row's design angle as the throttle comes back, and the chart shows whether either shaft passes its redline. The two-spool model only handles a choked nozzle; throttles where it unchokes are listed as not modelled.

Efficiencies. Overall efficiency is thrust power per fuel power. The model splits it into a thermal and a propulsive part using the jet's speed alone. When the jet leaves the nozzle above the outside pressure (a fixed convergent nozzle, especially high up), part of the thrust is that pressure push, which the speed-based split does not count, so the propulsive part can read above 100 %. It can also near zero thrust (around idle): the jet then barely outruns the flight, and the fuel's own mass, which the model counts as accelerated, dominates both the thrust and the energy gain. The overall figure is unaffected.

Numbers computed here agree with the command-line model to the last few digits. The browser uses a different maths library, and the project's test run checks that difference stays below a measured bound.
